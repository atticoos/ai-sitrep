Iran war’s unintended consequences have spread beyond the Middle East

People walk near a billboard depicting U.S. President Donald Trump on a building in Tehran, Iran, on August 22.

When US President Donald Trump launched his war against Iran, he called it an “excursion” that would take just a few weeks. Six months later, the conflict has become a nightmare for almost everyone even remotely involved in it.

Yet Trump’s war goals remain unfulfilled, even though the goalposts keep shifting: from initially demanding a complete Iranian surrender, regime change and the abandoning of the Islamic Republic’s nuclear ambitions, to more recently simply demanding the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and putting the brakes on Tehran’s nuclear program.

But the war’s unintended consequences have spread far beyond the Middle East, affecting global security, diminishing America’s global standing, and potentially providing its adversaries with an opportunity to expand their influence.

As the Trump administration focuses all its energy on trying to get out of the self-inflicted crisis in Iran, it has taken the eye off the ball of other potential global flashpoints the US has long been involved in, including Ukraine, Taiwan and the South China Sea, and the Koreas.

From Trump’s decision to scale back the annual joint military exercises with South Korea and pulling out the only US aircraft carrier in the Western Pacific, to the critical shortages of ballistic missile interceptors in Ukraine, many of the war’s most consequential geopolitical and security effects may ultimately be felt far beyond the Middle East, and have yet to materialize.

President Donald Trump listens as a reporter asks a question in the Oval Office of the White House on Thursday.

President Donald Trump remained vague Friday when asked about his campaign to choke off Iran’s economy, refusing to say whether he planned to sanction Chinese banks or if he’d discussed the matter with foreign counterparts.

The administration announced earlier this week Operation Economic Outcast, which threatened tough new sanctions on Iran’s trading partners if they didn’t cut off ties to Tehran.

But the administration stopped short of putting in place any new measures, and so far hasn’t listed any major financial institutions from China — Iran’s largest trading partner — as being subject to US sanctions.

Trump bristled when asked why he wasn’t applying new sanctions on Chinese banks doing business with Tehran.

“Who said I’m not? You don’t know if I’m doing it,” he said. “I don’t have to announce everything, do I?”

Later, asked which world leaders he’d discussed the pressure campaign with, Trump suggested it wasn’t on his agenda, even though Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Monday that Trump was placing calls to foreign counterparts “with specific requests to cease their interactions with the regime.”

“There’s not a lot to speak to. We don’t want to speak to them,” Trump said Thursday. “We’re not looking to meet or anything.”

“With the tools we have, we managed to restore about 40% of the damaged capacity. However, the damage that has been inflicted is very severe. It will definitely take at least 2 years of work just for those sites to be rebuilt,” Iran’s deputy oil minister, Ahmad Zeraatkar, told Fars on Thursday.

Iran-Oman Strait of Hormuz proposal sends oil prices lower: What we know

People spend time at a beach as commercial vessels are anchored in the Strait of Hormuz off Bandar Abbas, Iran, Wednesday, Aug. 5, 2026.

Iran and Oman discussed a “phased framework” for a joint project to create a temporary shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz. Oman’s foreign minister said he was “hopeful” the two countries would soon formalize the arrangement.

The proposal also includes a joint initiative to clear mines from the waterway. Technical negotiations are expected to continue as both countries work toward implementation.

Officials said talks will continue with the aim of reaching agreement on a permanent shipping corridor and the future administration of the strait.

Iran said the proposed deal would close a UN-authorized southern shipping route along Oman’s coast, which Iran has opposed. Vessels would instead travel through Iranian waters in both directions, Iranian media reported. An Omani statement did not mention closing the current route.

Qatar also discussed the proposal with Tehran and has publicly supported Omani-Iranian efforts to reach an agreement over the strait.

President Donald Trump said earlier that all mines in the Strait of Hormuz have been removed or detonated and warned that any vessel attempting to place new mines would be destroyed. He also said the United States was monitoring “every square inch” of the waterway.

Global oil prices have fallen for three consecutive days after Iran and Oman announced the framework. Futures of Brent crude, a major global benchmark, declined 3% to trade at almost $86 a barrel early today, while West Texas Intermediate, the North American benchmark, dropped 2.8% to $80 a barrel. Analysts said traders largely remain in “wait-and-see mode.”

The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world’s most important energy chokepoints.
