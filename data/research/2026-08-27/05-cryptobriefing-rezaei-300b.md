Iran sets conditions for reopening Strait of Hormuz, demands $300B in compensation

Mohsen Rezaei outlines sweeping list of demands including sanctions relief, military withdrawal, and wartime damages before restoring access to the world's most critical oil chokepoint

class="w-7 h-7 border border-foreground/30 text-foreground rounded-full flex items-center justify-center hover:bg-muted transition-colors"

Iran is putting a price tag on reopening the Strait of Hormuz, and it’s a steep one. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran’s Supreme National Security Council, announced that Tehran is compiling a formal list of conditions before it will restore passage through the waterway that, before the current conflict, carried roughly 20% of the world’s oil and LNG shipments.

The demands include up to $300 billion in compensation for wartime damages, the removal of US sanctions, the release of frozen Iranian assets potentially totaling $100 billion, a cessation of regional conflicts spanning Lebanon, Gaza, Yemen, and Iraq, and the lifting of the US naval blockade on Iranian ports.

Rezaei framed the conditions as a response to mediators who have been pressing Tehran to restore shipping access. Iranian officials have previously outlined six specific actions they want from Washington before any negotiations can move forward, chief among them a permanent halt to aggression and a full withdrawal of American military forces from the region.

Iran and Oman have been discussing a temporary shipping corridor that would share control and revenues between the two nations. But Iranian officials have been careful to condition even this limited arrangement on broader US concessions.

The Strait of Hormuz sits between Iran and Oman, with the navigable shipping lanes passing through waters claimed by both nations.

WTI crude sits at approximately $83, a price that reflects the sustained disruption. Before the conflict, the Strait of Hormuz handled approximately 20% of the world’s oil and liquefied natural gas shipments daily.

The frozen assets demand could prove equally thorny. Iranian funds held in various international accounts have been the subject of legal battles and diplomatic negotiations for years. Releasing $100 billion would require coordinated action across multiple jurisdictions, each with its own legal and political constraints.
