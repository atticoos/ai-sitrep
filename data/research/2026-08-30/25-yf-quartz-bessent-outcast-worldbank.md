U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Reuters on Sunday that the Treasury Department plans to roll out new secondary sanctions targeting Iran on a weekly basis, beginning with financial institutions.

"You're going to see a lot more of these every week," Bessent told Reuters. "We're starting with the banks, and we're telling the banks it's not okay to have Iranian money and to aid the regime."

Bessent made the remarks ahead of a Group of 20 finance leaders meeting, where he said he intends to press G20 finance ministers and central bank governors to sever economic ties with Iran or face secondary sanctions — penalties that can cut foreign banks and companies off from the U.S. financial system. "There can be no leakage," he said. "You're either with us or you're with the Iranians."

The comments came after Treasury imposed penalties last Friday on the United Arab Emirates branches of Egypt's Banque Misr over alleged financial links to Iran. Bessent said a possible next step would be cutting an institution off entirely from the dollar-based financial system.

The Treasury Department launched the sanctions campaign, called Operation Economic Outcast, last week, expanding the categories of Iran-related activity that can trigger secondary sanctions and immediately designating nearly 60 entities, individuals, and vessels across multiple jurisdictions. Treasury also issued new sectoral sanctions determinations covering digital assets, technology, gold, aviation, and shipping.

Among the entities sanctioned at the campaign's launch were a number of China- and Hong Kong-based companies and individuals, including Hong Kong-based Sweet Ocean Industrial Limited and several China-based individuals identified as Li Na, Tian Jianbai, and Zhang Limei.

Bessent said Sunday that he does not share the view held by some critics that the sanctions campaign is doomed unless Chinese companies are directly subjected to secondary penalties. He argued that China's ability to keep buying Iranian oil has been sharply limited by the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports, and that the stockpile of Iranian oil sitting in tankers continues to shrink, concluding: "Problem solved."

The U.S.-Iran conflict, now in its sixth month, has strained the global economy. The World Bank cut its global growth forecast for 2026 to 2.5%, calling the slowdown the worst hit to the global economy since the COVID-19 pandemic, as disruptions to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz pushed energy prices higher.
